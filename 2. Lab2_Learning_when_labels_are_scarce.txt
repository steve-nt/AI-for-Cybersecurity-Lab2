Lab 2: Learning When Labels Are Scarce 

1. What is this lab about? 
In real security work, labelling traffic is slow and expensive, so you usually have labels for only a 
small part of your data and a large unlabeled 
data. Semi-supervised learning (SSL) uses both the few 
labels and the unlabelled data together. In this lab you pretend you only have a few labels, use SSL 
to get value from the rest, and see how close you can get to your Lab 1 detector. 

You will work at three label budgets: 

• 1% labels — only a tiny slice is labelled. 
• 5% and 10% labels — a bit more, so you can see the trend. 
2. What you will be able to do afterwards 
• Set up a fair experiment where only a few labels are available. 
• Use unlabelled data to improve a detector (semi-supervised learning). 
• Compare against the right baselines so you can tell if it really helped. 
• Explain when semi-supervised learning helps and when it backfires.

5. Which methods to use 
Use pseudo-labelling (required), and one more semi-supervised method of your choice. 


Method What it is / beginner tip 
Pseudo-labelling (required) Train on the few labels, let the model guess the rest, keep only the 
confident guesses, and retrain. Simple and effective. 
Co-training (choose one) Split the features into two halves, train two models, and let each one teach 
the other with its confident guesses. Easiest of the three. 
Mean Teacher (choose one) A student model learns to agree with a slow, averaged teacher, even on 
slightly noised input. Needs a neural network. 
FixMatch (choose one) Keep a guess only when the model is confident on a lightly changed input, 
then make it agree on a strongly changed one. Most advanced, and 
changing table data is tricky, so explain your choice. 

Baselines cheat-sheet 

Always compare your SSL result against two lines: a model trained on the few labels only (the 
lower line SSL must beat), and your full-label Lab 1 model (the upper line SSL tries to approach). 
Without both, an SSL score means nothing. 

6. Step-by-step tasks 
Step 1: Recreate your baselines 

• Note your full Lab 1 score (all labels). This is the upper line. Then, at each budget (1%, 5%, 10%), 
train a plain model on only the labelled slice. This is the lower line. 
Step 2: Make the “few labels” splits 

• From the Lab 1 training set, keep the labels for 1%, 5%, and 10% of the rows (stratified, so the 
class balance stays the same). Hide the labels on the rest, this is your unlabelled pool. Fix the 
random seed. 
Step 3: Pseudo-labelling 

• Train on the labelled slice, guess the unlabelled pool, keep only the confident guesses, add 
them, and retrain. Repeat. Say what confidence cut-off you used. 
Step 4: One more method (your choice) 

• Add co-training, Mean Teacher, or FixMatch at each budget. Keep everything else the same as 
Step 3 so the comparison is fair. 
Step 5: Run one “change one thing” experiment 

What an ablation is 

Change exactly one thing, keep everything else the same, and see what happens to the scores. It 
shows which choices actually matter. 

• Pick one: (a) try a few confidence cut-offs 
• (b) use 25% / 50% / 100% of the unlabelled pool. Report the before/after numbers. 
Step 6: Compare your results: 

• Put all results in one table (accuracy, macro-F1, recall, ROC-AUC, FAR), and draw a curve of a 
metric against the label budget with the lower and upper lines shown. 

• Right metric: because the data is imbalanced, judge with macro-F1 and FAR, not accuracy 
alone. Also check recall so you do not miss attacks.

• Common mistakes to avoid 
• Skipping the few-labels-only baseline, then you cannot show that SSL helped. 
• Setting the confidence cut-off too low, so the model learns from its own bad guesses. 
• Judging only by accuracy while attacks get missed, always check recall and macro-F1.
